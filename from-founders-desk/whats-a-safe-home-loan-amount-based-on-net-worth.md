# What's a Safe Home Loan Amount Based on Net Worth?

  

  
    ![Mangesh Zope](../../assets/founder.jpeg)
    
      

Mangesh Zope

      

Founder, Peaceful Loans · IIM Calcutta Alumnus

    
  

  
  

A 38-year-old senior banker called us last quarter. Net worth: roughly ₹4.5 crore — split between mutual funds, EPF, ESOPs, and an inherited plot of land. He was looking at a ₹6 crore apartment in BKC and the bank had pre-approved him for ₹4.5 crore.

His question to me was sharp. *"Mangesh, my income easily supports the EMI. But my net worth is ₹4.5 crore and the loan is also ₹4.5 crore. Is this safe?"*

This is the question almost no salaried borrower thinks to ask, and almost no business owner forgets to ask. The answer is rarely covered in a home loan EMI calculator, because it has nothing to do with EMI.

It has to do with **what happens to you if everything goes wrong at once.**

## Why Net Worth Matters More Than Income for the Big Decision

Income tells you whether you can *service* the loan. Net worth tells you whether you can *survive* the loan if your income disappears.

A 25-year home loan is a long bet. In that quarter-century, the average person will go through at least one job change, possibly one career change, one major medical event in the family, and at least one significant market correction. Income protects you against the EMI. Net worth protects you against everything else.

This is why I find the salaried-vs-self-employed lens incomplete. The better lens is **income flexibility vs net worth depth**. Most failures in home loan decisions come from people who optimised for one and forgot the other.

## The Three Financial Safety Frameworks I Use

Across hundreds of advisory conversations with our ₹2 Cr+ ticket size customers, three frameworks come up repeatedly. I am laying them out together because no single one of them is sufficient on its own.

### Framework 1: Loan-to-Liquid-Net-Worth Ratio

This is the simplest and most powerful number nobody calculates.

**Loan amount ÷ Liquid net worth (excluding the property being purchased)**

Liquid net worth here means: cash, FDs, mutual funds, listed equity, gold, bonds — anything you can convert to money in 30 days without taking a serious haircut. EPF counts at a discount because of withdrawal restrictions. ESOPs at zero unless they are vested and tradeable.

A practical reading:

- **Below 1.5x** — comfortable. You have meaningful liquid backup.

- **1.5x to 3x** — manageable, but you are running thin. Build the cushion before you stretch further.

- **3x to 5x** — risky. One serious income disruption and you are forced into asset sales at the worst time.

- **Above 5x** — reckless unless you have a second household earner with a stable income.

The banker I mentioned was at 1x — loan equals liquid net worth. I told him to either reduce the loan to ₹3 crore (bringing the ratio to ~0.7x) or wait 18 months to build the liquid base further.

### Framework 2: Down Payment as a % of Net Worth

A separate but related question — how much of your net worth are you putting on the table at the moment of purchase?

For a ₹6 crore apartment with a ₹4.5 crore loan, the ₹1.5 crore down payment plus roughly ₹40-50 lakh of stamp duty, registration, brokerage, interiors, and shifting comes to ₹2 crore *upfront*. Out of a ₹4.5 crore net worth, that is 44%.

My rule of thumb here:

- Putting **20-30% of net worth** into the home purchase is healthy

- **30-50%** is acceptable if your income is strong and stable

- **Above 50%** means your portfolio just became a single-asset bet on Indian real estate

A 60-year-old has the right to do this. A 38-year-old doing it gives up two decades of compounding flexibility for a marginal lifestyle upgrade.

### Framework 3: The Six-Month, Twelve-Month, Twenty-Four-Month Test

Forget ratios for a second. Ask three plain questions:

**6 months without income — can I keep paying the EMI from liquid assets?**

**12 months without income — am I still solvent without selling property or breaking equity at a loss?**

**24 months without income — am I in trouble?**

If your answer to the first two is "yes" and to the third is "uncomfortable but survivable," you are in the right zone. If you fail at the 6-month mark, the loan is too big regardless of what your income suggests today.

This is the test that the Bengaluru case I'll write about separately fails. A software professional, eight years of clean payments, lost his job — and *three missed EMIs* triggered a SARFAESI notice. The flat sold for ₹95 lakh against an outstanding loan of ₹80 lakh. He walked away with ₹15 lakh after eight years of paying. The 6-month test, properly run earlier, would have either flagged a smaller loan or insisted on bigger emergency reserves.

## What Actually Counts as Net Worth

This sounds basic but it trips people up.

Real net worth is **assets you can convert to cash without forcing the rest of your life to collapse**. It is *not*:

- Equity in your current home that you would have to sell or rent to access

- ESOPs that are not yet vested or in a private company with no liquidity event

- Carry interest, deferred bonuses, or other promised future income

- Inherited property held jointly with siblings or parents

- The increased "fair value" of your business based on the last private round

I have seen too many founders count a ₹50 crore notional valuation of their own equity as net worth. When the home loan EMI hits and the business has a difficult quarter, that ₹50 crore does not show up to help. Liquid net worth is the only thing that shows up.

## Where the Property Itself Sits in This Calculation

Here is a counterintuitive point. **The property you are buying does not count as your safety net.**

This is because in a stress scenario, the property is illiquid (takes 6-12 months to sell in a normal market, longer in a bad one), the sale price under stress is typically 15-25% below market, and selling it defeats the purpose of buying it.

The Bengaluru auction story makes this concrete. A ₹1.2 crore apartment sold under SARFAESI for ₹95 lakh — a 21% haircut just from the forced-sale dynamic, before any market weakness.

So when you assess whether your net worth supports the loan, value the new property at zero in the safety calculation. It is an asset you live in, not an asset you can lean on.

## A Practical Net Worth Framework

Putting it all together, here is the simplified version I use in advisory calls:

| Factor | Comfortable Zone |

|---|---|

| Loan amount ÷ Liquid net worth | < 1.5x |

| Down payment + costs ÷ Total net worth | < 30% |

| Months of EMI covered by liquid reserves (post-purchase) | 12+ months |

| EMI ÷ Net monthly income | < 50% |

| Existing income earners in household | 2 (or 1 with substantial reserves) |

If you are comfortable on at least four of these five, you are in the safe zone. If you are stretched on three or more, you are taking on risk that does not show up in your EMI calculator.

## The Net Worth Question for Different Life Stages

The same loan-to-net-worth ratio means different things at different ages.

**Late 20s to mid 30s:** Net worth is small but income runway is long. A higher ratio (up to 3x) is defensible because you have 25-30 years of earning years to recover from any setback.

**Late 30s to mid 40s:** This is the riskiest stretch. Family obligations are high, parents may need support, children's education costs are rising, career risk is real. Keep ratios tighter (under 2x).

**Late 40s and beyond:** Income runway is shortening. A ₹3 crore home loan with 20 years tenure has you paying EMIs into your late 60s. Net worth depth becomes critical because the recovery window is short. Keep ratios below 1.5x and have substantial liquid reserves.

## What I Told the Banker

After the conversation, the banker did three things:

- Reduced the target property to ₹4.8 crore (from ₹6 crore)

- Took a ₹3.2 crore loan instead of ₹4.5 crore

- Used an OD-linked home loan structure to keep his liquid funds working as a buffer

Two years later, his business compensation has grown, his liquid net worth is up, and his loan-to-liquid-net-worth ratio is now under 0.5x. He has the option to upgrade later if he wants.

The friend who took the ₹4.5 crore loan at the same time? He is fine. Markets cooperated. But by his own admission, he doesn't sleep as well as he used to.

# How to Calculate the Maximum Home Loan You Can Actually Afford

  

  
    ![Mangesh Zope](../../assets/founder.jpeg)
    
      

Mangesh Zope

      

Founder, Peaceful Loans · IIM Calcutta Alumnus

    
  

  
  

A management consultant called us last quarter, frustrated. He had run his numbers through five different home loan calculators online. Each one gave him a different "maximum loan amount" — ranging from ₹1.4 crore to ₹3.2 crore on the same income inputs.

His question to me was straightforward. *"Mangesh, which calculator is right?"*

The honest answer: **all of them are technically right, and none of them is actually relevant to you.**

Online calculators answer one question — *what is the bank willing to lend you?* That is a useful number, but it is not the question you should be asking. The question you should be asking is — *what is the loan amount that I can comfortably absorb without my financial life unraveling over the next 20-25 years?*

These are very different numbers. Let me walk you through how to compute the second one.

## The Three Numbers That Matter

There are three distinct affordability numbers in any home loan decision. Most borrowers conflate them. Understanding the difference is the foundation of good borrowing.

### Number 1: Bank Eligibility (the calculator number)

This is what every online calculator gives you. Built on:

**Eligible EMI = (Net Monthly Income × FOIR%) − Existing EMIs**

Where FOIR (Fixed Obligations to Income Ratio) typically ranges from 40% to 65% based on income bracket. Higher income = higher FOIR allowed.

For a salaried professional with ₹3 lakh net monthly income and no existing EMIs:

- At 65% FOIR: Eligible EMI ₹1.95 lakh → Loan eligibility ~₹2.25 crore at 8.5%, 20 years

This is the *maximum* the bank will lend. It is not the amount you should target.

### Number 2: Comfortable Affordability (the number that matters)

This is what you can absorb without stressing your overall financial life. The right framework:

**Comfortable EMI = 35% to 45% of Net Monthly Income**

For the same ₹3 lakh net monthly income:

- At 40% comfortable EMI: ₹1.2 lakh → Loan ~₹1.39 crore at 8.5%, 20 years

- At 45% comfortable EMI: ₹1.35 lakh → Loan ~₹1.56 crore

So the comfortable affordability is **30-40% lower than the bank's eligibility number.** That gap is your safety margin.

### Number 3: Stress-Tested Affordability (the conservative anchor)

This is the loan amount that survives a difficult scenario — income reduction, family medical event, period of unemployment.

**Stress-tested EMI = 30% of Net Monthly Income, with 12 months of EMI in liquid reserves**

For the ₹3 lakh net monthly income:

- 30% comfortable EMI: ₹90,000 → Loan ~₹1.05 crore

- Reserve required: 12 × ₹90,000 = ₹10.8 lakh in liquid funds

This is the genuinely safe number, especially for borrowers in volatile industries (IT in restructuring, early-stage business, founder roles) or with single-income households.

## The Real Affordability Framework — Six Filters

Across hundreds of advisory conversations with our ₹2 Cr+ ticket size customers, I have arrived at a six-filter framework. Pass all six and your loan is comfortable. Fail any two and you are stretched.

### Filter 1: EMI as % of Net Income

The single most important number. Should be 35-45% for most situations. Above 50% is risky. Above 60% is the bank's max — almost never the right answer.

For IT professionals or others in volatile industries, target 35% rather than 45%.

### Filter 2: All EMIs Combined Below 50% of Net Income

This is the FOIR check, but applied conservatively. Total EMIs (home loan + car + education + credit card minimum dues) should be below 50% of net income. Banks allow up to 65% in some brackets. You should not.

### Filter 3: Liquid Net Worth at Least 1.5x of Loan Amount

Your liquid assets (cash, FDs, mutual funds, listed equity, gold) should be at least 1.5x the loan amount you're taking. This is your safety margin.

For a ₹2 crore loan, you should have at least ₹1.3 crore in genuinely liquid assets — *separate* from the down payment money. Below 1x is risky territory.

### Filter 4: 12 Months of EMI in a Dedicated Emergency Reserve

Not your investment portfolio. Not your kids' education fund. A dedicated, untouchable liquid reserve equal to at least 12 months of EMI.

For a ₹1.8 lakh EMI, that is ₹21.6 lakh sitting in a liquid mutual fund or sweep-in FD. Feels like dead money. It is the most important money in your portfolio for the next 20 years.

### Filter 5: Post-EMI Monthly Surplus Above 20% of Net Income

After paying EMI and handling all other fixed expenses (society, taxes, insurance, school fees, parents' support, utilities), you should have at least 20% of your net income left for savings, investments, and lifestyle.

If your post-everything surplus is below 15%, your loan is too big.

### Filter 6: Loan Ends by Age 60-62

This is the tenure filter. Pick a tenure where the loan ends before your typical retirement window — by age 60-62 for most. Going to age 65-70 with the bank's "maximum tenure" trick increases your risk meaningfully.

## A Concrete Calculation — The Right Way

Let me walk you through a real exercise for a 38-year-old professional with ₹3.5 lakh net monthly income, ₹50 lakh of existing liquid net worth, and no existing EMIs. Property of interest: ₹3 crore.

### Step 1 — Compute Bank's Eligibility (for reference)

- Net income: ₹3.5 lakh/month

- FOIR at 60% (high income bracket): ₹2.1 lakh available for EMI

- 20-year tenure at 8.5%: Eligibility ~₹2.43 crore

So the bank will likely offer up to ₹2.4 crore. **That is the ceiling, not the target.**

### Step 2 — Compute Comfortable Affordability

- Target EMI at 40% of net income: ₹1.4 lakh

- 20-year tenure at 8.5%: Loan amount ~₹1.62 crore

Already, this is ₹78 lakh below the bank's offer. Same income, very different loan size.

### Step 3 — Run the Six Filters Against ₹1.62 Crore Loan

Property cost: ₹3 crore

Loan amount: ₹1.62 crore (54% LTV)

Down payment + stamp duty + registration + brokerage: ₹1.5 crore upfront cash needed

Filter 1: EMI ₹1.4 lakh / ₹3.5 lakh = 40% ✅

Filter 2: All EMIs under 50% (no other EMIs) ✅

Filter 3: Liquid net worth ₹50 lakh / Loan ₹1.62 crore = 0.31x ❌

Filter 4: 12 months EMI = ₹16.8 lakh; reserve required ✅ (comfortably from ₹50 lakh)

Filter 5: Post-EMI surplus need 20% of ₹3.5 lakh = ₹70,000 ✅ (assuming reasonable other expenses)

Filter 6: 20-year tenure ends at age 58 ✅

Five out of six filters pass. The **liquid net worth filter (3) fails** — at 0.31x, the borrower is significantly under-buffered relative to the loan size.

### Step 4 — Recalibrate

Two options to fix Filter 3:

**Option A: Reduce loan size.** A ₹70-80 lakh loan would bring the ratio to 0.65x — still under 1x but much closer to safe. This means buying a smaller property (₹1.7 crore range) or arranging a larger down payment.

**Option B: Build liquid net worth before buying.** Wait 18-24 months, build liquid assets to ₹1.2-1.5 crore range, then buy at the original ₹1.62 crore loan size with ratio at ~1x.

**Option C: Pick a less expensive property.** A ₹2 crore property with the same financial profile means a smaller loan and a bigger down payment — both move ratios in the right direction.

Most customers facing this situation pick Option A or C — buying a smaller property than originally aspired to. Some pick Option B if the property market and family situation allow waiting.

What almost nobody should pick — *take the bank's full ₹2.4 crore offer despite failing Filter 3.* That is how stretched files start.

## What the Online Calculators Get Wrong

Three big issues with bank EMI calculators:

**1. They show maximum eligibility, not comfortable affordability.** The number designed to make you feel like you can afford more.

**2. They ignore property-related fixed costs.** Society maintenance, property tax, insurance — none of these are factored in. Real cost of ownership is 30-50% higher than the EMI alone.

**3. They don't stress-test for income disruption.** What looks affordable at full income often becomes unaffordable in a year of layoff or business downturn.

The right tool is not an online calculator. It is the six-filter framework above, run against *your actual numbers* including realistic stress scenarios.

## Building This Into a Buying Decision

A practical 5-step process to land on your real maximum:

**Step 1: Compute net monthly income honestly.** Not CTC. Take-home after tax, PF, professional tax.

**Step 2: Subtract realistic monthly expenses.** Use your last 6 months of bank statements to compute actual fixed expenses (society, school fees, EMIs, insurance, parents' support, helper salary, subscriptions). Add a 10% buffer for under-counted items.

**Step 3: Compute the EMI you can comfortably absorb.** Should be 35-45% of net income, leaving at least 20% surplus after everything.

**Step 4: Convert EMI to loan amount** based on the rate you expect (8-9%) and the tenure that ends by age 60.

**Step 5: Cross-check against the six filters.** If you fail two or more, either reduce the loan or build the missing piece (reserves, liquid net worth).

This whole exercise takes 90 minutes. It is the most valuable 90 minutes you can spend before signing a sanction letter.

## What I Tell Customers in Advisory Calls

Three principles I find myself repeating:

**1. The bank's maximum is not the target.** It is the ceiling, beyond which you cannot go. Your target should be 25-35% below it.

**2. The buffer matters more than the loan size.** A ₹1.5 crore loan with strong reserves is more comfortable than a ₹1.8 crore loan with thin reserves on the same income.

**3. The right loan amount lets you sleep, not stretch.** If the EMI math gives you anxiety on a Saturday morning, the loan is too big — regardless of what your eligibility says.

## What the Consultant Decided

After we did the exercise together, the consultant in question realised:

- His bank-eligibility figure was ₹2.4 crore — what the calculators had shown

- His comfortable-affordability figure was ₹1.6 crore

- His liquid net worth was thin relative to either number

He delayed the purchase by 14 months, used the time to grow his liquid reserves to ₹95 lakh (from ₹50 lakh), and eventually bought a ₹2.2 crore property with a ₹1.4 crore loan — well inside his comfortable range.

A year later, he had retained his savings rate, built reserves further, and was relaxed about the EMI. The compromise on the property size was negligible in retrospect; the compromise on financial peace would have been crushing.

## Peaceful Loans's Advise

The maximum home loan you can *afford* is almost always 25-35% lower than the maximum the bank will *lend*. The gap between these two numbers is your safety margin — and it is the difference between a comfortable 25-year journey and a stressful one.

Use the six-filter framework. Run it against your actual numbers, not the bank's. Don't let the calculator's "maximum eligibility" become the anchor for your decision.

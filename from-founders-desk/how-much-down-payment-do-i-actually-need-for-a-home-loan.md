# How Much Down Payment Do I Actually Need for a Home Loan?

  

  
    ![Mangesh Zope](../../assets/founder.jpeg)
    
      

Mangesh Zope

      

Founder, Peaceful Loans · IIM Calcutta Alumnus

    
  

  
  

A senior consultant called us last quarter, mid-property-search. He had been told by his bank that he needed "20% down payment" on a ₹3.5 crore property. He had ₹70 lakh ready. Felt comfortable.

His question — *"Mangesh, the math says ₹70 lakh down payment is enough for a ₹3.5 crore property at 20%. But my friends are saying I need much more. Are they being conservative or am I missing something?"*

He was missing something significant. The "20% down payment" headline that banks use for marketing is the principal-to-LTV calculation. The *actual cash* you need at the moment of property purchase is 35-45% of the property value when you add stamp duty, registration, brokerage, interiors, and other costs.

His friends were not being conservative. They had been through the experience and knew the real number.

This post is the practical map of what "down payment" actually means in Indian home buying, and how to size your cash reserves appropriately before you sign anything.

## What "Down Payment" Technically Means

The headline definition: **Down payment is the portion of the property cost you pay yourself, not financed by the home loan.**

Banks finance up to a certain percentage of the property value (the LTV, or Loan-to-Value ratio). The rest comes from your pocket. That "rest" is the down payment.

Per RBI's current LTV caps:

- **Loans up to ₹30 lakh:** Maximum 90% LTV (so minimum 10% down payment)

- **Loans ₹30-75 lakh:** Maximum 80% LTV (so minimum 20% down payment)

- **Loans above ₹75 lakh:** Maximum 75% LTV (so minimum 25% down payment)

For most ₹2 Cr+ ticket size customers, the relevant slab is the third one — **25% down payment minimum.**

But this is just the loan-related down payment. The actual cash needed at purchase is much higher.

## The Five Components of "Real Down Payment"

When you buy a property, your upfront cash needs include:

### Component 1: Down Payment Proper

The amount not covered by the home loan, per LTV cap. For a ₹3 crore property with maximum 75% LTV — bank funds ₹2.25 crore, you put up ₹75 lakh.

### Component 2: Stamp Duty

State government charge for registering the property in your name. Varies by state and gender:

- **Maharashtra:** 5% (Mumbai 6% in some categories)

- **Karnataka:** 5-5.6%

- **Delhi:** 4-6% (4% if registered in woman's name)

- **Tamil Nadu:** 7%

- **Gujarat:** 4.9%

- **Haryana:** 5-7% (concession for women in urban areas)

For a ₹3 crore property in Mumbai, stamp duty is ~₹15-18 lakh.

### Component 3: Registration Charges

Separate from stamp duty. Typically 1% of property value, capped at ₹30,000 in some states.

For a ₹3 crore property: ~₹30,000 to ₹3 lakh.

### Component 4: Brokerage

If you bought through a real estate broker, typically 1-2% of property value, paid by the buyer (sometimes split with the seller).

For a ₹3 crore property: ~₹3-6 lakh.

### Component 5: Society Transfer / Move-In Costs

For resale properties — society transfer fees, share certificate transfer, water/electricity meter transfer, name change in society records. Typically ₹50,000 to ₹2 lakh.

For under-construction — possession charges, society formation costs, and sometimes pro-rated maintenance for the first 12 months.

## The Total Cash Picture

Putting this together, for a ₹3 crore property in Mumbai:

| Component | Amount |

|---|---|

| Down payment (25% LTV) | ₹75,00,000 |

| Stamp duty (5%) | ₹15,00,000 |

| Registration (1%) | ₹3,00,000 |

| Brokerage (1.5%) | ₹4,50,000 |

| Society/move-in costs | ₹1,50,000 |

| **Total upfront cash needed** | **₹99,00,000** |

**That is 33% of property value, not 25%.**

Add interiors and furnishing (a separate but real budget), and the total can easily reach 40-50% of property value.

## The Interior Cost Most Borrowers Forget

This is the cost most borrowers underestimate. For a new ₹3 crore apartment, basic functional interiors typically cost ₹15-30 lakh:

- **Modular kitchen:** ₹4-10 lakh

- **Wardrobes (2-3 bedrooms):** ₹3-6 lakh

- **Living/dining furniture:** ₹3-8 lakh

- **Painting and minor electrical:** ₹2-5 lakh

- **AC installation (3-4 units):** ₹3-5 lakh

- **Lighting, accessories, basic appliances:** ₹3-6 lakh

For premium projects with bare-shell delivery, interiors can easily run ₹40-60 lakh.

For a ₹3 crore property, expect ₹20 lakh interior budget at minimum. **This needs to be planned separately from the down payment.**

## The Three Down Payment Strategies

Across hundreds of property purchases we have advised, three strategies emerge:

### Strategy 1: Minimum Down Payment (25%)

Take the maximum loan the bank's LTV allows. Down payment is the minimum 25% (for ₹75 lakh+ loans).

**When this makes sense:**

- You have other high-return investment opportunities (equities, business)

- The home loan rate is low relative to your alternative investment returns

- You want to preserve liquidity for other goals

**When this doesn't make sense:**

- You don't have alternative investment discipline (the surplus money will get spent on lifestyle)

- Your CIBIL and income aren't strong enough to get the best rate (so the loan cost is high)

- You are uncomfortable with maximum debt exposure

### Strategy 2: Moderate Down Payment (30-40%)

A common middle ground. You put more down to reduce loan size and EMI, while still preserving meaningful liquidity for other goals.

**When this makes sense:**

- Your monthly cash flow benefits more from a lower EMI than from preserved liquidity

- You are in a volatile income segment (IT in restructuring, founder)

- You want to keep total household debt below 4x annual income

This is the most commonly chosen strategy by ₹2 Cr+ customers we work with.

### Strategy 3: Maximum Down Payment (50%+)

You put down half or more of the property value. Loan is small, EMI is light.

**When this makes sense:**

- You are buying primarily as an investment (rental yield + appreciation)

- You want to be debt-free quickly

- You have substantial liquid net worth and can afford the concentration

- You are nearing retirement and want minimal mortgage stress

**When this doesn't make sense:**

- You have higher-return investment opportunities and are sacrificing them

- Your liquid reserves drop to thin levels after the down payment

- You could service a larger EMI comfortably and benefit from tax deductions

## The Hidden Trade-Off Most Borrowers Don't See

Here is a less-obvious consideration. A higher down payment reduces your loan amount. A smaller loan amount means:

- Lower EMI

- Lower total interest paid over loan life

- **Lower Section 24 tax benefit** (since interest is the deductible portion)

Section 24 allows up to ₹2 lakh per year of home loan interest deduction (under old tax regime, for self-occupied property). If your loan is small enough that your annual interest is below ₹2 lakh, you "waste" the unused tax cap.

For most ₹2 Cr+ home loans, the annual interest in Year 1 is ₹15-25 lakh — far above the ₹2 lakh cap. So this trade-off rarely matters for premium borrowers, but it does matter for smaller loans.

## Where Should the Down Payment Money Come From?

Three common sources, in order of preference:

### Source 1: Existing Liquid Savings

Cash, FDs, liquid mutual funds you can convert to money in 7-15 days. This is the cleanest source. No tax implications, no transaction costs.

### Source 2: Long-Term Investment Liquidations

Equity mutual funds held for 12+ months, listed equity holdings, gold. Liquidating these involves:

- Capital gains tax (10% on equity LTCG above ₹1 lakh per year)

- Market timing risk (if you have to sell when markets are down)

- Transaction costs

Plan these liquidations carefully, ideally over 3-6 months to avoid forced selling at one moment.

### Source 3: Family Contributions

Gifts from parents, grandparents, or close family. Indian Income Tax Act treats gifts from specified relatives as tax-free.

For documentation purposes, banks generally want to see:

- A clear gift deed or bank-traceable transfer

- Gifter's identity confirmation

- Source of gifter's funds (especially for large amounts)

Don't structure family contributions as informal cash transfers — banks will query untraceable inflows in your account.

### Sources to Avoid

**Personal loans for down payment.** Banks will explicitly reject this — they don't want a customer servicing two loan EMIs from Day 1. Some borrowers try to disguise this by taking a personal loan months before the home loan; CIBIL catches it.

**Liquidating retirement funds.** EPF / PF withdrawal for housing is allowed under specific conditions (you need to be employed for 3+ years), but it should be a last resort. Retirement corpus, once depleted, is hard to rebuild.

**Borrowing from informal sources.** Family loans (vs gifts) can become messy, especially if the home loan also fails or property doesn't appreciate as expected.

## How to Plan Your Down Payment Timeline

A practical sequence for serious property purchases:

**18-24 months before:**

- Identify your target property type and price range

- Compute total cash needed (down payment + stamp duty + registration + brokerage + interiors)

- Begin systematically saving toward this amount

**12-18 months before:**

- Build 80% of total cash in a mix of liquid funds and short-term FDs

- Maintain 20% in equity funds for slight return uplift (only if 12+ months from purchase)

**6-12 months before:**

- Move 60-70% of total cash into liquid funds or sweep-in FDs (zero market risk)

- Begin shortlisting properties and getting pre-approval from banks

**3-6 months before:**

- All funds in liquid form, accessible within 7-15 days

- Bank pre-approval in hand

- Documentation ready

This timeline accommodates property identification and offer-to-registration timing without forcing distressed asset sales.

## What I Told the Consultant

We worked through the actual numbers for his ₹3.5 crore target:

| Component | Amount |

|---|---|

| Down payment at 25% LTV | ₹87,50,000 |

| Stamp duty in his state (5.5%) | ₹19,25,000 |

| Registration (1%) | ₹3,50,000 |

| Brokerage (1.5%) | ₹5,25,000 |

| Society + move-in | ₹2,00,000 |

| Interiors budget | ₹25,00,000 |

| **Total cash needed** | **₹1,42,50,000** |

His ₹70 lakh covered 49% of what he actually needed. Either he had to:

- **Increase the down payment funding** (delay purchase by 12-18 months to save more)

- **Reduce the property target** to ₹2.4 crore range (his ₹70 lakh covers 50% of total cash for that price)

- **Stretch the loan** to higher LTV (limited by RBI cap)

- **Defer interiors** (live in basic shell for 12 months while saving)

He chose a combination — reduced his target to ₹2.8 crore, deferred premium interiors to ₹15 lakh budget, and waited 8 months to build up cash reserves further. He bought 11 months later with comfortable margins and is now interior-finishing in phases.

The alternative — pushing through with ₹70 lakh against ₹3.5 crore target — would have left him forced into a personal loan for interiors, drained reserves, and stretched financially through Year 1 and 2 of the EMI.

## Peaceful Loans's Advise

The "20-25% down payment" is the loan-related minimum, not your actual cash need. For a serious home purchase in Indian metros, plan for **35-45% of property value as upfront cash** including stamp duty, registration, brokerage, and basic interiors.

Build this cash reserve methodically over 12-24 months in liquid instruments. Don't drain emergency funds, don't take personal loans, don't liquidate retirement corpus.

The wait of 12-18 months to build the right reserves is almost always worth it. The alternative — pushing through with thin reserves and ending up financially stretched — creates years of stress that no property is worth.

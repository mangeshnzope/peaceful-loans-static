# Can I Buy a House Without a Home Loan? Should I?

  

  
    ![Mangesh Zope](../../assets/founder.jpeg)
    
      

Mangesh Zope

      

Founder, Peaceful Loans · IIM Calcutta Alumnus

    
  

  
  

A successful business owner came to us last quarter with an unusual question. He had just sold a portion of his business equity for ₹8 crore. He was looking at a ₹4 crore apartment in BKC. His instinct was to pay cash, fully — no home loan, no EMI, no bank involvement.

His question to me — *"Mangesh, am I being financially smart? Or am I being emotionally driven? Friends are telling me I'm leaving money on the table by not taking a loan."*

This is one of the most under-discussed financial decisions in Indian home buying. Most articles you'll read assume you *will* take a home loan and discuss how to optimise it. Few discuss whether you should take one at all.

The honest answer: it depends on three specific factors. And the conventional wisdom — *"home loans are always financially smart because of tax benefits"* — is more nuanced than it first appears.

## The Core Trade-Off

Let me set up the math cleanly. When you have the cash to buy a property fully but choose to take a home loan instead, here is what is happening:

**You are deploying borrowed capital at the home loan interest rate to free up your own capital, which can then be invested elsewhere.**

This makes financial sense **if** the return you can earn on your invested capital exceeds the cost of the home loan after accounting for tax benefits.

The math:

- **Home loan rate (cost of borrowing):** ~8.5% currently

- **Effective rate after Section 24 tax benefit:** ~7.5-8% (depending on tax bracket and how much of your interest falls within the ₹2 lakh annual cap)

- **Required investment return to break even:** approximately 8-9% net of tax

For an average equity portfolio earning 10-12% pre-tax over the long term, the math favours taking the loan and investing your own cash. For someone whose alternative use of the cash is FDs at 7-7.5%, the math is closer to neutral.

This is the standard argument for "always take the loan." It works for many situations. But it doesn't work for all of them.

## The Three Scenarios Where Cash Purchase Makes Sense

### Scenario 1: You Have a Specific, Lower-Return Use for the Cash

If your alternative to using cash for the property is keeping it in FDs or a savings account, the home loan economics break down. The 8.5% home loan rate is meaningfully higher than the 7-7.5% FD return. After tax, FDs may give you 5-5.5% net. Borrowing at 8% to invest at 5% is wealth-destructive.

**When does this happen in practice?**

- You are conservative about investments and don't want equity exposure

- You are already heavily invested in equities and want to reduce concentration risk

- You are nearing retirement and shifting to capital preservation

For these profiles, paying cash for the property is genuinely the better financial decision.

### Scenario 2: You Are Self-Employed With Volatile Income

A founder, consultant, or business owner with irregular income faces a different calculation. The "comfortable EMI" capacity isn't easy to compute. A bad business quarter could turn an EMI obligation into stress.

For these profiles, owning the property outright eliminates a fixed monthly obligation that can become problematic when business cash flow is lumpy. The peace of mind has real value, even if the math marginally favours taking the loan.

### Scenario 3: You Are Older (50+) and Want Simplicity

If you are 50+ and your career runway is shortening, taking a 25-year home loan that extends into your 70s is questionable. Even a 15-year loan extends into your mid-60s.

For older borrowers, the simplification of being mortgage-free can be worth giving up the marginal investment return. Estate planning is also cleaner — heirs inherit a clean property, not an outstanding loan.

## The Three Scenarios Where Loan Makes Sense Despite Having the Cash

### Scenario 1: You Have Strong Equity Returns Available

If your investment discipline is solid and your alternative is a well-diversified equity portfolio earning 10-12% pre-tax, the home loan math works favorably. Borrow at 8.5%, invest at 11%, net win of 1.5-2% per year on the investment portfolio.

For a ₹2 crore loan freed up over 20 years, the cumulative wealth differential can be ₹40-80 lakh.

### Scenario 2: You Want Liquidity Optionality

Once you sink ₹4 crore into a property, that capital is locked. You cannot easily access it without selling the property or taking a loan against it. **Money in liquid investments, by contrast, is accessible whenever needed** — for opportunities, emergencies, business reinvestments.

For people who genuinely value liquidity and have the discipline to keep that liquidity working, taking the loan preserves optionality.

### Scenario 3: You Want Forced Savings Discipline (Yes, Really)

This is counter-intuitive. Some borrowers actually save *more* with a home loan than without one, because the EMI is a non-negotiable monthly outflow that builds equity over time.

If you don't have strong savings discipline, the EMI essentially forces you to save. The "freed-up" cash from not paying the loan upfront often gets consumed in lifestyle inflation rather than being invested.

This is a behavioural argument, not a mathematical one — but for many people, it is the truthful one.

## The Hidden Cost of "No Loan"

Three considerations often missed in the cash purchase decision:

### Cost 1: Loss of Section 24 Tax Benefit

For self-occupied property under the *old* tax regime, you can deduct up to ₹2 lakh per year of home loan interest. For someone in the 30% tax bracket, this saves ₹60,000 per year in taxes — over 20 years, ₹12 lakh.

If you have no loan, no tax benefit. Note: This benefit is **not available under the new tax regime**.

### Cost 2: Concentration Risk

A ₹4 crore cash purchase means ₹4 crore of your net worth is now in a single illiquid asset. If that property's value declines (Indian metros can have 5-10 year flat or declining cycles), you have no diversification.

A loan-funded purchase keeps more of your wealth in liquid, diversified investments — reducing the concentration risk on your home.

### Cost 3: Insurance / Risk Management

A home loan typically comes with mandatory property insurance. A cash purchase leaves you to organise this yourself, which many borrowers forget — leaving the property uninsured against fire, earthquake, flood for years.

(Worth noting: this insurance is small in cost and easy to arrange separately, so it's a minor point. But it does get missed.)

## The Real Question — Where Will the Cash Sit?

The financial logic comes down to one question. If you don't use the cash for the property, **where does it actually go?** Be honest with yourself.

**If the answer is** — "into a diversified equity portfolio, where it will compound at 10-12% over 20 years" — taking the loan is mathematically better.

**If the answer is** — "into FDs and a savings account, where it will earn 6-7%" — paying cash for the property is mathematically better.

**If the answer is** — "into 'investments' I might tinker with, sometimes well, sometimes badly" — the math depends on your actual track record. Don't model based on hopes; model based on history.

## What About Partial Cash + Partial Loan?

For someone who has substantial cash but isn't comfortable with full leverage, a middle path makes sense — pay 50-60% in cash, take a smaller home loan for the rest.

Benefits:

- **Lower EMI** is comfortable from Day 1

- **Section 24 tax benefit** still available on the smaller loan's interest

- **Some liquidity preserved** for investments and emergencies

- **Lower concentration risk** on the property

For our ₹2 Cr+ ticket size customers, this 50-60% cash + 40-50% loan structure is often the most balanced answer for those with substantial liquidity.

## A Concrete Comparison

Let me put numbers on three paths for someone with ₹4.5 crore liquid net worth, looking at a ₹4 crore property:

### Path A: Full Cash Purchase

- ₹4 crore goes to property + closing costs of ~₹40 lakh

- Net liquid net worth post-purchase: ₹10 lakh

- Annual EMI: ₹0

- 20-year wealth picture (assuming 6% property appreciation, residual ₹10 lakh in FD): ₹13 crore in property + ₹35 lakh in FD = ₹13.35 crore

### Path B: 50% Cash + 50% Loan

- ₹2 crore down payment + ₹40 lakh closing = ₹2.4 crore from liquid

- ₹2 crore home loan at 8.5%, EMI ₹1.74 lakh/month

- Net liquid net worth post-purchase: ₹2.1 crore (assuming all kept liquid)

- 20-year wealth picture (₹2.1 crore in equities @ 10%, property @ 6% appreciation, EMIs paid from income):

- Property value: ₹13 crore

- Equity portfolio: ~₹14 crore

- Total: ₹27 crore (but minus 20 years of EMIs of ~₹4 crore total)

- Net: ~₹23 crore

### Path C: Full Loan (Maximum LTV)

- ₹3 crore home loan at 8.5% (maximum 75% LTV)

- ₹1 crore down + closing = ₹1.4 crore from liquid

- Net liquid net worth post-purchase: ₹3.1 crore

- 20-year wealth picture (₹3.1 crore in equities @ 10%, property @ 6%, EMIs from income):

- Property: ₹13 crore

- Equity portfolio: ~₹21 crore

- Total: ₹34 crore (minus 20 years of EMIs of ~₹6 crore total)

- Net: ~₹28 crore

So mathematically, the maximum-loan path produces the highest wealth — *if* the equity portfolio actually delivers 10% returns and you maintain the EMI discipline.

But the maximum-loan path also has the highest variance — bad equity years compound into the cumulative wealth differently than good years. The 50% cash path gives up some upside for considerably less risk.

## What I Told the Business Owner

For the business owner I mentioned at the start, we ran the framework:

- Liquid net worth post-business sale: ₹8 crore

- Property target: ₹4 crore

- Investment alternative: He had a discipline of investing in his own business-related opportunities (returns historically 15-20%, but with volatility) plus diversified equities (10-12%)

- Income: Lumpy from his businesses, no salary

For him, we recommended **partial cash + partial loan**:

- ₹1.5 crore down payment + ₹40 lakh closing

- ₹2.4 crore home loan over 15 years (since he was 49)

- Use SBI Maxgain OD product to park excess cash flow against the loan

- Keep ₹6 crore liquid for business and equity investments

This gave him:

- **Manageable EMI** (~₹2.36 lakh/month, well within his cash flows)

- **Section 24 tax benefit** (₹2 lakh/year deductible)

- **Liquidity preserved** for business opportunities

- **OD account flexibility** for his lumpy income pattern

A year later, he had used his liquid pool to fund a business expansion that returned ~25% in the first year — significantly outperforming the home loan rate. Had he sunk the full ₹4 crore in cash into the property, he would have missed that opportunity.

## Peaceful Loans's Advise

You can absolutely buy a house without a home loan. Whether you should depends on three honest questions:

**1. What is the alternative use of your cash?** Equities at 10-12% favours taking a loan. FDs at 6-7% favours paying cash.

**2. How important is liquidity to you?** Lumpy income, business owner, near-retirement borrowers benefit more from preserved liquidity.

**3. What is your savings discipline like?** If a home loan EMI is the only way you save consistently, taking the loan has behavioural benefits beyond pure math.

For most ₹2 Cr+ customers we work with, a **partial cash + partial loan structure** turns out to be the right answer — usually 50-60% down, with the rest as a comfortable home loan. Pure cash purchase has merit for specific scenarios, particularly older borrowers and those without alternative high-return investment use.

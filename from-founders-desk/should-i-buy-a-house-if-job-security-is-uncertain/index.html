Should I Buy a House If Job Security is Uncertain?

Mangesh Zope

Founder, Peaceful Loans · IIM Calcutta Alumnus

A senior IT manager at a Tier-1 services company asked me this question recently over a Saturday afternoon call. He had been planning a ₹3 crore apartment purchase for two years. Then his team had layoffs in three consecutive quarters. Two of his peers — both in their 40s — had been let go. He had survived.

His question to me was honest. "Mangesh, I have the eligibility. I have the down payment. But should I commit to a 25-year EMI when I am not sure my job will be here in 25 months?"

I have written separately on the broader market data — the polarising sentiment between business owners and the salaried IT segment is real and well documented. But that piece was about the market. This one is personal. What should you do?

Here is the framework I walked him through.

The Wrong Frame: "Should I Buy?"

The first thing I told him: don't ask yourself whether to buy. Ask yourself how to buy in a way that survives the job risk you are worried about.

The decision to buy a house is rarely the issue. The issue is the structure of the purchase. The same job risk that makes a stretched ₹3 crore loan dangerous is perfectly survivable on a ₹2 crore loan with the same income.

This is the conversation banks will not have with you. Banks pre-approve based on your current eligibility. They do not pre-approve based on what your eligibility will be if your industry restructures.

That is your job.

The Five Questions to Run Through Honestly

Job uncertainty is not one risk — it is three risks bundled together: layoff risk, salary stagnation risk, and re-employment-time risk. The questions below tease them apart.

1. If I lose my job tomorrow, how many months of EMI can I pay from liquid reserves alone?

Not "from my income." From cash, FDs, liquid mutual funds — money you can access in a week without taking a haircut. The honest minimum here is 12 months of EMI. Below 6 months, you are one bad quarter away from a SARFAESI notice — and yes, banks really do issue them after 90 days of non-payment, as we have seen play out publicly in recent months.

2. If I am unemployed for 9-12 months, how does my new salary compare to my current one?

Many laid-off senior professionals get re-hired at 20-30% lower compensation, especially in industries undergoing AI-driven restructuring. If your EMI is calibrated to your current peak salary, a re-employment hit becomes a permanent EMI stress.

3. Is my spouse's income enough to service the EMI alone for 12-18 months?

Two-earner households have a fundamentally different risk profile. If your spouse can carry the EMI on her income for a year, your job risk becomes a manageable scenario instead of a catastrophic one.

4. Is my industry shrinking, restructuring, or growing?

This is uncomfortable to answer honestly. Indian IT services is restructuring. Investment banking has flat hiring. D2C is consolidating. Healthcare and renewables are growing. Be honest with yourself about which side of the curve your role sits on.

5. Can I move cities or countries if needed for re-employment?

A house ties you down geographically. If you might need to move for a new job, an owned property becomes a liability — you can't easily sell it, and renting it out at the EMI-equivalent yield is rarely possible in Indian metros.

If your answers are uncomfortable on three or more of these, you are not ready for the loan size you are considering. Reduce the loan or postpone the purchase. Neither is a defeat.

The Three Real Options When Job Risk Is High

Once you have run the questions, you usually find yourself in one of three positions. Here is what to do in each.

Position A: You Want to Buy, Risk is Genuine

This is the most common case. You want the home, the family wants the home, prices are not going to wait — but the job risk is real.

The right structure is:

  • **Smaller loan than your eligibility.** Cap EMI at 35-40% of net income, not the bank's 60-65% ceiling.
  • **OD-linked home loan structure** (like SBI Maxgain or HDFC's overdraft variant). Park excess cash in the OD account — it reduces interest cost while preserving liquidity. We wrote about this product separately. For job-uncertain borrowers, this product is genuinely transformational.
  • **Term life insurance** equal to the outstanding loan amount. Pure-term, not the home loan insurance the bank pushes. Annual premium for ₹2 crore cover for a healthy 35-year-old is ₹15,000-20,000.
  • **A separate emergency corpus** of 12-18 months of EMI sitting in a liquid fund. Not the down payment money. Not your wedding savings. A separate corpus.

This combination de-risks job loss almost completely for the first 12-18 months, which is enough runway for re-employment in most cases.

Position B: Genuinely on the Bubble at Work

If you are not sure your job will exist in 6-12 months — performance issues, restructuring underway, your skills being deprecated — postpone. Renting for one more year is not a financial loss. Buying into a 25-year EMI you can't service is.

The opportunity cost of "missing" this property cycle is much smaller than the cost of a forced sale 18 months later.

Position C: Substantial Reserves, Stable Spouse Income

If your liquid net worth comfortably covers the loan and you have a second household earner, your job risk is much smaller than it feels. Don't let market anxiety drive you to under-buy. Just buy intelligently — same OD structure, same term insurance, same conservative EMI ratio.

The Mistake I See Most Often

Smart professionals over-react to job uncertainty in one of two opposite directions:

  • They **abandon the home purchase entirely** out of fear, often paying 20% more in rent over the next 5 years and missing genuine appreciation
  • They **stretch beyond reason** because "real estate prices keep going up" and use future expected income to justify present overcommitment

Both are wrong. The honest middle is — buy a property within your conservative serviceable range with a structure that survives a 12-18 month income gap.

What About Renting Forever?

This question always comes up.

In Indian metros, the rental yield on residential property is currently 2-3% per year. Home loan interest rates are 8.25-8.75%. So in pure cash flow terms, renting is mathematically cheaper than owning for most properties — at least in the first 7-10 years.

The case for owning is not financial. It is:

  • Stability and emotional ownership
  • Forced savings (your EMI builds equity over 20-25 years)
  • Insulation from rental hikes (which run 8-10% annually in Tier-1 cities)
  • Tax benefits under Section 24 and 80C

If your job is genuinely unstable, renting in a top location for 2-3 more years and buying with stronger reserves later is often the smarter call. The math will not punish you for waiting.

What I Told the IT Manager

He went away and ran the five questions. Honestly, he flunked three of them — emergency reserves were thin, his industry was restructuring, his spouse's income alone wouldn't cover the EMI.

He is now doing three things:

  • Building 18 months of EMI reserves before he buys (target: complete in 14 months)
  • Looking at a slightly smaller property (₹2.4 crore instead of ₹3 crore)
  • Planning to use an OD-linked structure when he does buy

He has not given up on the dream. He has just structured it to survive whatever the next 25 years throw at him.

Peaceful Loans's Advise

Job uncertainty does not automatically mean don't buy. It means don't buy in a way that requires uninterrupted employment for the next 25 years.

Most borrowers who run into trouble were not victims of layoffs. They were victims of loan structures that had no margin for layoffs. The structure is what fails — not the buyer.
